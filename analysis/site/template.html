Key finding

Players are not born in proportion to births. A belt from Merseyside and Lancashire across to South Yorkshire produces the most players per boy born, and the south coast the fewest. Within regions, boys born closer to any professional club were more likely to reach the top two divisions. Deprivation and urbanisation do not explain the differences once region is included, and nor does distance to an elite Category 1 academy.

Abstract

English football spends heavily on finding young players, yet it is not known whether the players who reach the top two divisions are born across England in proportion to births or in particular places. We ask where English-born men who played in the Premier League or the Championship were born, compared with where boys were born. From Wikidata and Wikipedia we identify {{nStudy}} men born 1975–2005 with evidence of a league appearance at one of {{nClubsTopTwo}} top-two clubs since 1992; {{nEnglish}} were born in England and {{nMapped}} can be placed on a map. Expected counts come from {{birthsTotal}} million male births estimated from ONS mid-year populations. Players are {{regionRatio}} times as common per birth in London ({{londonRate}} per 10,000 male births) as in the South West ({{swRate}}), and {{nRegionsSig}} of 9 regions differ from England at p < 0.05. Within regions, birthplaces are more clustered than births at distances up to 60 km (inhomogeneous L-function, DCLF {{lDCLFall|p}}; births-only null {{lNullAll|p}}). Across Travel to Work Areas (TTWAs) the Empirical Bayes Moran’s I is {{moranTTWA}} ({{moranTTWAp|p}}), with high values in a belt from Merseyside and Lancashire across to South Yorkshire and low values along the south coast, but only one area ({{giTopName}}, q = {{giTopQ}}) comes close to passing a false discovery rate correction. In a negative binomial model with region effects, each extra 10 km to the nearest professional club lowers the rate by {{distPct}}% (rate ratio {{distRR}}, 95% CI {{distLo}}–{{distHi}}, {{distP|p}}), while deprivation and urban share have no effect once region is included. For the {{nAcadPlayers}} players born 1996–2005, who were of academy age after the 2012 academy reform, distance to any club matters (rate ratio {{clubYoungRR}}) and distance to an elite Category 1 academy does not ({{acadRR}}, {{acadP|p}}). Of all {{nStudy}} study players, {{shareOutsideEngland}}% were born outside England, mostly in Western Europe and the other UK nations. The results describe areas, not individuals.

Scope note

The results are associations between areas, not proof of cause: club locations also reflect where population and industry were. Players and their club histories come from Wikidata and Wikipedia, which are community-edited; a validation sample of the first eligibility pass found {{valFIpct}}% false inclusions, which led to the rule that requires a recorded league appearance. Players are placed where they were born, usually the town of a maternity hospital, so Travel to Work Areas are used as the main areas. The births baseline is a proxy built from ONS population estimates, and deprivation is measured in 2025. The results should not be used to judge places, communities or individual children.

Findings
1

A regional belt, not isolated hotspots

London ({{londonRate}}) and the North West ({{nwRate}}) produce the most players per 10,000 boys born, the South West ({{swRate}}) and the South East ({{seRate}}) the fewest. The pattern works at the scale of regions and city-regions: region effects remove the spatial autocorrelation from the models.

2

Weak evidence for any single area

Liverpool is the most productive area, with {{liverpoolO}} players against {{liverpoolE}} expected. The local tests find a gradient of moderately elevated areas, and only {{giTopName}} comes close to passing a correction for testing all {{nTTWA}} areas.

3

Distance to a club matters

Each extra 10 km to the nearest professional club lowers the rate by {{distPct}}% (95% CI {{distPctLo}}–{{distPctHi}}%). The effect is significant in {{nRobustSig}} of {{nRobust}} model variants and stronger for players born 1990–2005 (rate ratio {{distRRYoung}}).

4

Deprivation is a proxy for region

Without region effects, deprivation seems to raise production (rate ratio {{imdRRmOne}} per 10 IMD points, {{imdPmOne|p}}). With them the effect disappears ({{imdRR}}, {{imdP|p}}): the North is both more deprived and more productive.

5

Not elite academies

For the {{nAcadPlayers}} players born 1996–2005, distance to any club has a strong effect (rate ratio {{clubYoungRR}}), and distance to a Category 1 academy none ({{acadRR}}, {{acadP|p}}). The route into the professional game appears to start at the nearest club.

6

Half the players were born abroad

{{shareOutsideEngland}}% of study players were born outside England. Relative to population, {{topHeadName}} leads with {{topHeadRate}} players per million people. Among players born outside the UK, Europe’s share rose from {{eurOld}}% to {{eurNew}}% between the cohorts.
